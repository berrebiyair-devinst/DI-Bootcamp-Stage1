Making Large Language Models More Efficient
A comparative review of LoRA, QLoRA, GPTQ and AWQ
Mini-project 1 | Full-Stack & AI Bootcamp | Research meta-analysis report
Scope: Four published studies (2022–2024). This is a qualitative comparative synthesis, not a statistical meta-analysis of pooled effect sizes.
1. Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) are Transformer-based systems trained on large text datasets. They can answer questions, generate code and support applications such as educational assistants. However, larger models need more GPU memory, storage and computing power. In my bootcamp, I learned about model parameters, quantization, GGUF, llama.cpp, benchmarks and local versus cloud deployment. This made me interested in a practical question: how can developers use powerful LLMs without extremely expensive hardware?
This report compares four research papers on efficient LLM adaptation and inference: LoRA (Hu et al., ICLR 2022), QLoRA (Dettmers et al., NeurIPS 2023), GPTQ (Frantar et al., ICLR 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., MLSys 2024). The shared theme is reducing the computational and memory cost of LLMs while preserving useful quality. The papers address two related but different tasks: fine-tuning a model and running a model for inference.
2. Paper Summaries
2.1 LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models
Citation: Hu, E. J., Shen, Y., Wallis, P., Allen-Zhu, Z., Li, Y., Wang, S., Wang, L., & Chen, W. (2022). LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR).
Problem and method. Traditional fine-tuning updates all the parameters of a pretrained model, which is costly. LoRA keeps the original Transformer weights frozen and trains small low-rank matrices added to selected layers. In simple terms, it learns a small adjustment instead of changing the entire model.
Evidence and evaluation. The authors tested RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2 and GPT-3 on natural-language understanding and generation tasks, including GLUE, E2E, DART and WebNLG; metrics include task accuracy and generation measures such as BLEU. For GPT-3 175B, they report up to 10,000 times fewer trainable parameters and about three times less GPU memory than full fine-tuning, with comparable or better task quality in their experiments.
My assessment. LoRA is attractive for adapting one base model to several applications. However, it still requires a pretrained base model and its results depend on the chosen tasks, adapter settings and model architecture.
2.2 QLoRA — Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs
Citation: Dettmers, T., Pagnoni, A., Holtzman, A., & Zettlemoyer, L. (2023). QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 36).
Problem and method. LoRA reduces trainable parameters, but the base model still consumes memory. QLoRA combines frozen 4-bit base-model weights with trainable LoRA adapters. It introduces NormalFloat 4-bit (NF4), double quantization and paged optimizers to manage memory.
Evidence and evaluation. The paper studies LLaMA and T5 model families, multiple model sizes and eight instruction datasets, including OpenAssistant data. It evaluates instruction-following/chat quality using Vicuna-style comparisons and human or model-based judgments. The authors report fine-tuning a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU and a Guanaco 65B result of 99.3% of ChatGPT’s score on their Vicuna evaluation. This percentage is specific to that benchmark, not a general measure of intelligence.
My assessment. QLoRA is especially relevant when GPU memory is limited. Still, a 48 GB GPU is far beyond my laptop, and chatbot evaluations can be subjective or difficult to reproduce exactly.
2.3 GPTQ — Accurate Post-Training Quantization
Citation: Frantar, E., Ashkboos, S., Hoefler, T., & Alistarh, D. (2023). GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR).
Problem and method. Even after training, large models can be expensive to store and serve. GPTQ compresses an already-trained Transformer by quantizing weights to around 3–4 bits, using approximate second-order information to reduce the resulting error. It is mainly an inference optimization, not a fine-tuning method.
Evidence and evaluation. The study evaluates GPT-style models, including OPT, on language-modeling benchmarks using perplexity and downstream task accuracy, alongside GPU inference measurements. The authors report quantizing a 175B model in about four GPU hours, with small quality degradation, and inference speedups of about 3.25× on A100 and 4.5× on A6000 GPUs in tested settings.
My assessment. GPTQ helps developers deploy large models with less memory. The speedup is hardware- and implementation-dependent; it should not be assumed for my Intel laptop CPU.
2.4 AWQ — Activation-aware Weight Quantization
Citation: Lin, J., Tang, J., Tang, H., Yang, S., Chen, W.-M., Wang, W.-C., Xiao, G., Dang, X., Gan, C., & Han, S. (2024). AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization for On-Device LLM Compression and Acceleration. Proceedings of Machine Learning and Systems (MLSys 6).
Problem and method. Not all model weights contribute equally to output quality. AWQ uses observed activations to identify important weight channels and scales them before low-bit quantization. Unlike some alternatives, it avoids backpropagation and expensive weight reconstruction.
Evidence and evaluation. The authors test LLM families including LLaMA and instruction-tuned models on language-modeling and domain benchmarks, measuring perplexity, accuracy and inference throughput. They report that protecting roughly 1% of salient weights substantially reduces quantization error, and an optimized inference system provides more than 3× speedup over a Hugging Face FP16 implementation on tested desktop and mobile GPUs.
My assessment. AWQ is useful for practical model serving, especially where memory bandwidth matters. But its measured benefits depend on GPU kernels and devices, so a local CPU deployment needs separate testing.
3. Comparative Analysis
Paper	Main goal	Core technique	Evaluation focus
LoRA	Low-cost adaptation	Freeze base; train low-rank adapters	GLUE, E2E, DART, WebNLG; quality and memory
QLoRA	Memory-efficient adaptation	4-bit frozen base + LoRA; NF4	Instruction datasets, Vicuna/human judgments; memory
GPTQ	Cheaper inference	Post-training weight quantization	Perplexity, task accuracy, GPU speed
AWQ	Efficient inference	Activation-guided weight scaling	Perplexity, task accuracy, GPU throughput
Main similarity: all four methods try to preserve model usefulness while reducing resource requirements. Their important difference is the stage of the workflow: LoRA and QLoRA focus on adaptation, while GPTQ and AWQ focus on compression for inference. Their architectures are generally pretrained Transformer models rather than completely new language-model families.
Training and evaluation are not directly comparable. LoRA and QLoRA measure adaptation quality and memory efficiency; GPTQ and AWQ examine quantization error and inference performance. Their model sizes, datasets, GPU hardware and evaluation protocols differ. Therefore, comparing a 3× memory reduction with a 3× inference speedup would be misleading: these numbers measure different things.
Strengths and reproducibility. All four papers describe concrete algorithms and provide public implementations, making them useful for further experiments. However, reproducing published numbers may require specific GPUs, model versions, calibration datasets, software kernels and benchmark settings. QLoRA also highlights uncertainty in chatbot evaluations. A fair comparison should run the same base model, task set and hardware across methods.
4. Insights and Reflection
The clearest trend is that efficiency is becoming a major part of LLM research. The goal is not only to build larger models but also to make existing models cheaper to customize and deploy. Another trend is combining techniques: QLoRA builds on the idea of LoRA and adds quantization. GPTQ and AWQ show that post-training compression can improve deployment feasibility.
For a bootcamp developer, QLoRA seems especially promising for adapting a model when cloud GPU memory is limited, while AWQ and GPTQ are relevant when the goal is to serve responses efficiently. I would not choose one method only because of a headline benchmark. I would check the license of the base model, evaluate quality on my application’s questions, measure latency, and calculate the total cloud or local cost.
Common challenges include quality loss at lower precision, evaluation differences, limited reproducibility across hardware, and the gap between benchmark scores and real user experience. Future research could improve CPU-friendly quantization, combine low-cost fine-tuning with faster inference, standardize evaluations, and make efficient methods work better across languages and smaller devices.
My practical takeaway connects directly to my local readiness audit: I have 16 GB RAM, Ubuntu through WSL2 and an AVX2-capable Intel CPU. A quantized 7B model is plausible to test locally, but these papers do not prove it will be fast on my machine. I would start with a smaller GGUF model in llama.cpp, record memory usage and response time, and then compare it with a cloud GPU.
5. Conclusion
These four papers show that efficient LLM development involves two separate decisions: how to adapt a model and how to run it. LoRA and QLoRA reduce the cost of fine-tuning; GPTQ and AWQ reduce the cost of inference through quantization. The studies support the idea that useful model quality can often be maintained with much lower resource requirements, but results depend on hardware and evaluation methods. As a Full-Stack and AI student, I would use these techniques as practical options rather than automatic solutions, and validate them with a small real-world test before integrating an LLM into a SaaS.
